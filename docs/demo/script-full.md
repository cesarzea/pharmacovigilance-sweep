# Full walkthrough script (about 9 minutes, narrated)

The long version of [script.md](script.md): what the sweep does, how it does it, and how it is built.
Same conventions: screen, caption (also the subtitle), narration at about 2.5 words a second. Frames
marked *slide* are rendered by `scripts/make_demo_frames.py`; *diagram* slides are drawn by the same
script (boxes and arrows, no external renderer); *shot* frames reuse `frames/shots/`.

## Part 1 · The problem (about 1 min 30)

| # | Screen | Caption | Narration | ~s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | slide · title card | A Microsoft AI Foundry, Snowflake and Databricks POC | Pharmacovigilance Sweep: an agentic proof of concept on Microsoft Foundry, with Snowflake or Azure Databricks as the data platform. In the next nine minutes: what it does, how it does it, and how it is built and measured. | 14 |
| 1 | slide · five channels and the 15-day clock | Side effects are mentioned where pharmacovigilance does not look | A pharmacovigilance department only sees what reaches it. Side effects are mentioned every day in medical information calls, in sales reps' visit notes, in patient support programmes run by vendors, in quality complaints and on patient forums. Under the EU rules, GVP Module VI, the company is responsible for all of them. | 20 |
| 2 | slide · the clock: day 0, 15 days serious, 90 days non-serious, forwarding timeline | The clock starts when anyone in the company first hears of the case | The regulatory clock does not start when pharmacovigilance opens the case. It starts the day anyone in the company, or a vendor working for it, first learns of the minimum information: a patient, a reporter, a product and a reaction. Fifteen days for a serious case, ninety for the rest. A vendor also has a contractual forwarding timeline, usually one business day. | 22 |
| 3 | slide · two jobs side by side: reconciliation vs re-screening | Two distinct jobs: did PV receive everything, and is there anything nobody passed on? | Two questions follow. First: did pharmacovigilance receive everything the channels and vendors say they sent? That is a reconciliation of logs, deterministic, no AI needed. Second: is there anything in the source text that nobody passed on? That needs reading, judgement and a person at the end. | 18 |
| 4 | slide · spontaneous vs solicited (ICH E2D) | Spontaneous or solicited changes what "reportable" means | The channel also decides what reportable means. A medical information call or a sales note is a spontaneous report: reporting implies suspicion. A patient support programme or a planned review of patient forums is an organised data collection system: the report is solicited, and it becomes a case only after a causality assessment says a relationship is at least possible. The sweep carries that distinction through every step. | 24 |

## Part 2 · What it does (about 2 min 30)

| # | Screen | Caption | Narration | ~s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | slide · the six steps | Re-read every record. Keep what PV never received. | The sweep re-reads the source records after the fact and takes each one through six steps: screen, extract, apply the rules, match, produce a finding, and reconcile. Let me take them one by one. | 13 |
| 6 | slide · step 1 screening: input text, output class and criteria, example | Step 1 · Screen: adverse event, special situation, or nothing for PV? | Screening is one call to the small model with a strict JSON schema. It answers whether the text describes an adverse event, a special situation such as an overdose or a pregnancy, or nothing for pharmacovigilance, and which of the four minimum criteria are present. It quotes the evidence verbatim. Records with nothing for PV stop here, except a random sample that a person checks. | 22 |
| 7 | slide · step 2 extraction: the fields, evidence quotes, escalation to the large model | Step 2 · Extract the case facts, every one with its quote | Extraction turns the text into case facts: patient with sex, age and initials; reporter type; products and which one is suspect; events with a proposed term; seriousness criteria; dates; and a causality statement when the reporter gave one. Every field carries the sentence it came from. If the small model's answer does not validate, the record is escalated to the large model once. | 22 |
| 8 | slide · step 3 rules: ownership, seriousness with the IME list, day 0, deadline, forwarding status | Step 3 · Deterministic rules: no model decides a deadline | The rules are code, not a model. Is it our product? Is it serious, including the important medical events list? What is day zero, and so what is the regulatory deadline and how many days remain? Was the vendor's forwarding timeline met? A model may propose; the clock is computed. | 18 |
| 9 | diagram · step 4 matching: blocking in SQL → candidates → agent with five read-only tools → terminal tool | Step 4 · Match: has pharmacovigilance already got this case? | Matching asks whether PV already holds the case. First, deterministic blocking in SQL finds candidates by substance, patient and dates. Then an agent, with read-only tools over the safety database, the intake queue and the vendor log, reads the candidates, searches further if it needs to, and returns one of five outcomes through a strict tool call: new, already in the safety database, already in the intake queue, significant follow-up, or non-significant follow-up. | 26 |
| 10 | slide · the initials lesson: round 2 vs round 3 by scenario kind | Why patient initials matter: 0.69 to 0.84 and 0.84 to 0.96 | One lesson from the evaluation: without a patient identifier, a safety database with ten thousand cases on four substances is full of lookalikes, and the agent accepted them. Adding patient initials, which real safety databases hold and public data does not, took matching from sixty-nine to eighty-four percent with GPT and from eighty-four to ninety-six with Claude. | 20 |
| 11 | slide · step 5 finding: priority formula and the case intake package fields | Step 5 · A finding with a priority and a proposed intake package | Everything new, every significant follow-up and everything the agent could not resolve becomes a finding. A priority score ranks it: serious first, then overdue, at risk, forwarding breached, causality required. Each finding carries a proposed case intake package: the facts, the dates, the clock, the match, the evidence. | 18 |
| 12 | slide · step 6 reconciliation: vendor log vs intake log, three statuses | Step 6 · Reconcile what vendors sent against what PV received | Reconciliation is the deterministic job: vendor transmission logs against the pharmacovigilance intake log, by reference and date. Sent and received, sent and never received, received but never logged by the vendor. In the synthetic data, fifty items were planted as never received, and all fifty are found. | 17 |
| 13 | shot · 06 review app queue | The reviewer works a prioritised queue | The reviewer works the queue in a review app that runs inside the data platform. Each finding shows the package, the source text with the evidence highlighted, the rules, the agent's justification and its tool calls. | 14 |
| 14 | shot · 07 decision recorded | Confirm day 0, choose the outcome, record the decision | A checklist mirrors the regulatory questions. The reviewer confirms day zero and picks one of four outcomes: not reportable, already received, follow-up needed, or valid case. A valid case writes the confirmed package and, when the deadline was missed or the vendor was late, a compliance finding with a root cause. Nothing is closed by a model. | 20 |

## Part 3 · How it is built (about 3 min)

| # | Screen | Caption | Narration | ~s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | diagram · context: reviewer, engineer, GitHub, Azure (job, Foundry), data platform, public datasets | Three systems, one repository, everything deployed by script | The architecture has three systems. The data platform, Snowflake or Databricks, holds all data and all decisions. Microsoft Foundry holds the models, the hosted matching agents, the content filters and the evaluations. Azure runs the sweep as a container job. One repository holds the code, and every resource comes from its scripts. | 20 |
| 16 | diagram · data model: schemas REF, SRC, PV, AGENT_API, SWEEP, REVIEW, AUDIT, REPORT, OPS with arrows | Nine schemas: sources, the safety database, results, review, audit | The data model separates what came in from what the sweep produced and what people decided. Sources and the safety database extract on one side; the sweep's results and the audit trail of every model call on another; reviewer decisions and compliance findings on a third; and report views on top. Migrations are versioned and applied once each. | 20 |
| 17 | shot · 05 Snowflake AGENT_API views | The agent's credential can read four secure views and nothing else | The matching agent gets its own service user. It can read secure views over the safety database summary, the case history, the intake queue and the vendor log, and nothing else. It never writes. The runner has a second user with write access to results only, and the review app a third. | 17 |
| 18 | shot · 03 Foundry deployments | One Foundry project, two providers, no API keys | On the Foundry side, one project carries both providers: GPT in the EU data zone, Claude as Global Standard. Models, quota and the marketplace attestation for Claude are all parameters in Bicep. Everything is called with Entra ID; API key authentication is disabled on the resource. | 18 |
| 19 | shot · 04 Foundry hosted agents | The matching agent is a hosted agent in Foundry Agent Service | The matching agent runs as a hosted agent: a container image, built from the repository and pulled by the project's identity, one agent per provider with the same code. Each invocation carries one record's payload and returns the decision. Sessions are reused, so a warm call costs about a second of platform overhead. | 19 |
| 20 | diagram · the hosted agent: entry point implements the protocol → matcher → tools → platform; secrets from a project connection | Secrets never sit in the image: a project connection injects the agent's key at start | The agent's data credential, the private key of its service user, lives in a Foundry project connection and is injected into the sandbox at start. The entry point implements the hosting protocol directly, so no model has to echo a ten-thousand-token payload into a tool call. That detail mattered: the first version did, and Claude truncated it. | 21 |
| 21 | diagram · the sweep runner: Container Apps job, Key Vault, identities, concurrency, audit rows | The sweep runs unattended as a container job | The runner is a Python package with three backends behind one interface: a local DuckDB file for development, Snowflake, and Databricks. In Azure it runs as a Container Apps job with a user-assigned identity; its data keys come from Key Vault. Every model call, every agent invocation and every rule result is written as a row, with tokens, latency and cost. | 21 |
| 22 | slide · guardrails and prompt injection: untrusted text, read-only tools, content filter test | Source text is untrusted: read-only tools, validation twice, content filters | Forum posts and notes are untrusted input. The agent has no tool that writes or sends anything; every output is validated against a schema and against the payload; and the default content filter was tested with overdose and self-harm texts, which must reach assessment and did, unblocked. | 17 |
| 23 | shot · 09a Databricks catalog | The same platform on Azure Databricks: same tables, results copied, nothing recomputed | The whole platform was ported to Azure Databricks in one afternoon: the same schemas in Unity Catalog, the same runner and agents reading it, the same review app as a Databricks App. The processed results were copied from Snowflake rather than recomputed. One SQL directory per platform; everything else is shared. | 19 |
| 24 | shot · 09b Databricks app reconciliation | Same numbers on both platforms | The proof is the review app on Databricks showing the same reconciliation numbers as on Snowflake, and the hosted agents answering from the Foundry sandbox against either platform. | 12 |

## Part 4 · How it is measured (about 1 min 30)

| # | Screen | Caption | Narration | ~s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | slide · synthetic data design: 13 scenario kinds, 5 channels, anchored on FAERS cases, two generators | 450 synthetic records with known answers, anchored on real cases | There is no public dataset for this job, so the evaluation set was built scenario first: four hundred and fifty records across thirteen scenario kinds and five channels, each with its ground truth, ninety-three anchored on real FAERS cases so that lookalikes are real. Half the texts were written by GPT and half by Claude, so neither grades its own prose. | 22 |
| 26 | shot · 08 Foundry evaluation report | Custom evaluators in Foundry grade routing, matching and causality | Three custom code evaluators are registered in the Foundry project and grade every run: routing, matching outcome and causality statement. The same functions run on the laptop, so a number in a document can always be reproduced. | 15 |
| 27 | slide · results table round 3, both providers, plus latency and cost | Measured, not assumed | On the same records: screening recall above ninety-seven percent for both providers with no false positives; extraction valid on every record; matching eighty-four percent with GPT and ninety-five with Claude; a full sweep of four hundred and fifty records costs about eleven dollars in model calls either way. | 19 |
| 28 | slide · what the numbers do not say: scale of proof, tier fairness, synthetic text | What the numbers do not say | And what they do not say: the texts are synthetic, GPT ran on a small model throughout while Claude matched with its large one, and the hosted path is proven on samples. The business case keeps those caveats next to the figures. | 15 |
