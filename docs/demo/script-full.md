# Full walkthrough script (about 16 minutes, narrated)

The long version of [script.md](script.md): what the sweep does, how it does it, how it is built, how it
is measured and what it is worth. Same conventions: screen, caption (also the subtitle), narration at
about 2.5 words a second. Frames marked *slide* are rendered by `scripts/demo_full_frames.py`; *diagram*
slides are drawn by the same script (boxes and arrows, no external renderer); *shot* frames reuse
`frames/shots/`. Every figure is traceable to a document in the repository (see the production notes).

## Part 1 · The problem (about 1 min 40)

| # | Screen | Caption | Narration | ~s |

| 0 | slide · title card | A Microsoft AI Foundry, Snowflake and Databricks POC | Pharmacovigilance Sweep: an agentic proof of concept on Microsoft Foundry, with Snowflake or Azure Databricks as the data platform. In the next sixteen minutes: what it does, how it does it, how it is built and measured, and what it is worth. | 15 |
| 1 | slide · five channels and the 15-day clock | Side effects are mentioned where pharmacovigilance does not look | A pharmacovigilance department only sees what reaches it. Side effects are mentioned every day in medical information calls, in sales reps' visit notes, in patient support programmes run by vendors, in quality complaints and on patient forums. Under the EU rules, GVP Module VI, the company is responsible for all of them. | 20 |
| 2 | slide · the clock: day 0, 15 days serious, 90 days non-serious, forwarding timeline | The clock starts when anyone in the company first hears of the case | The regulatory clock does not start when pharmacovigilance opens the case. It starts the day anyone in the company, or a vendor working for it, first learns of the minimum information: a patient, a reporter, a product and a reaction. Fifteen days for a serious case, ninety for the rest. A vendor also has a contractual forwarding timeline, usually one business day. | 22 |
| 3 | slide · two jobs side by side: reconciliation vs re-screening | Two distinct jobs: did PV receive everything, and is there anything nobody passed on? | Two questions follow. First: did pharmacovigilance receive everything the channels and vendors say they sent? That is a reconciliation of logs, deterministic, no AI needed. Second: is there anything in the source text that nobody passed on? That needs reading, judgement and a person at the end. | 18 |
| 4 | slide · spontaneous vs solicited (ICH E2D) | Spontaneous or solicited changes what "reportable" means | The channel also decides what reportable means. A medical information call or a sales note is a spontaneous report: reporting implies suspicion. A patient support programme or a planned review of patient forums is an organised data collection system: the report is solicited, and it becomes a case only after a causality assessment says a relationship is at least possible. The sweep carries that distinction through every step. | 24 |

## Part 2 · What it does (about 3 min 30)

| # | Screen | Caption | Narration | ~s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | slide · the six steps | Re-read every record. Keep what PV never received. | The sweep re-reads the source records after the fact and takes each one through six steps: screen, extract, apply the rules, match, produce a finding, and reconcile. Let me take them one by one. | 13 |
| 6 | slide · step 1 screening: input text, output class and criteria, example | Step 1 · Screen: adverse event, special situation, or nothing for PV? | Screening is one call to the small model with a strict JSON schema. It answers whether the text describes an adverse event, a special situation such as an overdose or a pregnancy, or nothing for pharmacovigilance, and which of the four minimum criteria are present. It quotes the evidence verbatim. Records with nothing for PV stop here, except a five percent sample, ranked deterministically by hash so it can be reproduced, that a person checks. | 24 |
| 7 | slide · step 2 extraction: the fields, evidence quotes, escalation to the large model | Step 2 · Extract the case facts, every one with its quote | Extraction turns the text into case facts: patient with sex, age and initials; reporter type; products and which one is suspect; events with a proposed term; seriousness criteria; dates; and a causality statement when the reporter gave one. Every field carries the sentence it came from. If the small model's answer does not validate, the record is escalated to the large model once. | 22 |
| 8 | slide · step 3 rules: ownership, seriousness with the IME list, day 0, deadline, forwarding status | Step 3 · Deterministic rules: no model decides a deadline | The rules are code, not a model. Is it our product? Is it serious, including a list of important medical events? The proof of concept ships a short demo list; the EMA list is brought by the sponsor. What is day zero, and so what is the regulatory deadline and how many days remain? Was the vendor's forwarding timeline met? And after matching, a record pharmacovigilance already holds gets no new clock: on the GPT run, the overdue count fell from one hundred and eighty-one to one hundred and thirty-six once those stopped counting. A model may propose; the clock is computed. | 30 |
| 9 | diagram · step 4 matching: blocking in SQL → candidates → agent with five read-only tools → terminal tool | Step 4 · Match: has pharmacovigilance already got this case? | Matching asks whether PV already holds the case. First, deterministic blocking in SQL finds candidates by substance, patient and dates. Then an agent, with read-only tools over the safety database, the intake queue and the vendor log, reads the candidates, searches further if it needs to, and returns one of five outcomes through a strict tool call: new, already in the safety database, already in the intake queue, significant follow-up, or non-significant follow-up. | 26 |
| 10 | slide · the initials lesson: round 2 vs round 3 by scenario kind | Why patient initials matter: 0.69 to 0.84 and 0.84 to 0.96 | One lesson from the evaluation: without a patient identifier, a safety database with ten thousand cases on four substances is full of lookalikes, and the agent accepted them. Adding patient initials, which real safety databases hold and public data does not, took matching from sixty-nine to eighty-four percent with GPT and from eighty-four to ninety-six with Claude. | 20 |
| 11 | slide · the error analysis: six causes, the accuracy trajectory, the by-kind table | Every disagreement was classified; the fixes came from the causes | Every matching disagreement was classified by cause before anything was changed. In the first round there were thirty-seven: blocking that cut the true case out of the candidates, the agent dismissing a candidate it had been offered after its own search returned nothing, lookalikes, the right case with the wrong follow-up label, debatable gold labels, and rate limits. Accuracy went from seventy-one to ninety-one percent with a country and date rule, fell to sixty-nine when an exact-key pass offered lookalikes to every record, and reached eighty-four and ninety-six with the initials. The by-kind table shows where GPT still loses: cases already in the safety database and follow-ups. The behaviour behind it is visible in the tool calls: Claude confirms a candidate by reading the case history; GPT rejects it when its own search finds nothing. | 46 |
| 12 | slide · step 5 finding: priority formula and the case intake package fields | Step 5 · A finding with a priority and a proposed intake package | Everything new, every significant follow-up and everything the agent could not resolve becomes a finding. A priority score ranks it: serious first, then overdue, at risk, forwarding breached, causality required. Each finding carries a proposed case intake package: the facts, the dates, the clock, the match, the evidence. | 18 |
| 13 | slide · step 6 reconciliation: vendor log vs intake log, three statuses | Step 6 · Reconcile what vendors sent against what PV received | Reconciliation is the deterministic job: vendor transmission logs against the pharmacovigilance intake log, by reference and date. Sent and received, sent and never received, received but never logged by the vendor. In the synthetic data, fifty items were planted as never received, and all fifty are found. | 17 |
| 14 | shot · 06 review app queue | The reviewer works a prioritised queue in a five-page app | The reviewer works the queue in a review app that runs inside the data platform. Each finding shows the package, the source text with the evidence highlighted, the rules, the agent's justification and its tool calls. The app has five pages: the queue, the reconciliation, the channel KPIs, a model comparison built from the audit trail, and the QC sample of screened-out records. | 22 |
| 15 | shot · 07 decision recorded | Confirm day 0, choose the outcome, record the decision | A checklist mirrors the regulatory questions. The reviewer confirms day zero and picks one of four outcomes: not reportable, already received, follow-up needed, or valid case. A valid case writes the confirmed package and, when the deadline was missed or the vendor was late, a compliance finding with a root cause. Nothing is closed by a model. | 20 |

## Part 3 · How it is built (about 4 min 40)

| # | Screen | Caption | Narration | ~s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 16 | diagram · context: reviewer, engineer, GitHub, Azure (job, Foundry), data platform, public datasets | Three systems, one repository, everything deployed by script | The architecture has three systems. The data platform, Snowflake or Databricks, holds all data and all decisions. Microsoft Foundry holds the models, the hosted matching agents, the content filters and the evaluations. Azure runs the sweep as a container job. One repository holds the code, and every resource comes from its scripts. | 20 |
| 17 | diagram · data model: schemas REF, SRC, PV, AGENT_API, SWEEP, REVIEW, AUDIT, EVAL, REPORT, OPS | Ten schemas: sources, the safety database, results, review, audit, evaluation | The data model separates what came in from what the sweep produced and what people decided. Ten schemas: sources and the safety database extract on one side; the sweep's results and the audit trail of every model call on another; reviewer decisions and compliance findings on a third; the gold labels and metrics of the evaluation; and report views on top. Migrations are versioned and applied once each. | 22 |
| 18 | shot · 05 Snowflake AGENT_API views | The agent's credential can read six secure views and nothing else | The matching agent gets its own service user. It can read six secure views: the safety database summary, the case history, the intake queue, the intake log, the vendor log and the product list, and nothing else. It never writes. The runner has a second user with write access to results only. The review app runs under the owner role, because Snowflake does not let a Streamlit app change hands; that limitation is written down. | 24 |
| 19 | shot · 03 Foundry deployments | One Foundry project, two providers, no API keys | On the Foundry side, one project carries both providers: GPT in the EU data zone, Claude as Global Standard. Models, quota and the marketplace attestation for Claude are all parameters in Bicep. Everything is called with Entra ID; API key authentication is disabled on the resource. | 18 |
| 20 | shot · 04 Foundry hosted agents | The matching agent is a hosted agent in Foundry Agent Service | The matching agent runs as a hosted agent: a container image, built from the repository and pulled by the project's identity, one agent per provider with the same code. Each invocation carries one record's payload and returns the decision. Sessions are reused, with a fresh agent thread for every invocation so that one record never sees another, and a warm call costs about a second of platform overhead. | 23 |
| 21 | diagram · the hosted agent: entry point implements the protocol → matcher → tools → platform; secrets from a project connection | Secrets never sit in the image: a project connection injects the agent's key at start | The agent's data credential, the private key of its service user, lives in a Foundry project connection and is injected into the sandbox at start. The entry point implements the hosting protocol directly, so no model has to echo a ten-thousand-token payload into a tool call. That detail mattered: the first version did, and Claude truncated it. | 21 |
| 22 | diagram · the sweep runner: Container Apps job, Key Vault, identities, concurrency, audit rows | The sweep runs unattended as a container job, and nothing fails silently | The runner is a Python package with three backends behind one interface: a local DuckDB file for development, Snowflake, and Databricks. In Azure it runs as a Container Apps job with a user-assigned identity; its data keys come from Key Vault. Every model call is written as a row with tokens, latency and cost; every agent invocation with its latency and its tool calls. The agent's own tokens are not exposed by the framework, so the matching cost is an estimate, and the documents say so. And nothing fails silently: a transport error, an exhausted rate limit or an unparseable reply becomes a blocked decision that goes to a reviewer. | 36 |
| 23 | slide · guardrails and prompt injection: untrusted text, read-only tools, content filter test | Source text is untrusted: read-only tools, validation twice, content filters | Forum posts and notes are untrusted input. The agent has no tool that writes or sends anything; every output is validated against a schema and against the payload; and the default content filter was tested on the screening calls with overdose and self-harm texts, which must reach assessment and did, unblocked. | 18 |
| 24 | slide · seven roles, no update or delete, Databricks without key material, the append-only gap | Least privilege: seven roles, and no run-time role can update or delete | Least privilege is not three users but seven roles. An owner role used only for deployment. The pipeline, which writes results and audit. The agent, which reads its six views. The review app. Reviewers, who see patient fields unmasked. Analysts, who see report views only, masked by design. And a CI role with rights on a cloned database. No run-time role can update or delete anything, except the runner closing its own run row. On Databricks there is no key material at all: runner and agents authenticate with Entra tokens. One gap is stated honestly: Unity Catalog has no insert-only privilege, so append-only is not enforced there yet, and the fix is documented. | 40 |
| 25 | shot · 09a Databricks catalog | The same platform on Azure Databricks: same tables, results copied, nothing recomputed | The whole platform was ported to Azure Databricks: the same schemas in Unity Catalog, the same runner and agents reading it, the same review app as a Databricks App. The SQL was verified offline with a local Spark and Delta before a workspace existed, and passed at the first real run. The processed results were copied from Snowflake rather than recomputed. One SQL directory per platform; everything else is shared. | 24 |
| 26 | shot · 09b Databricks app reconciliation | Same numbers on both platforms | The proof is the review app on Databricks showing the same reconciliation numbers as on Snowflake, and the hosted agents answering from the Foundry sandbox against either platform. | 12 |
| 27 | slide · six decision records with their alternative and reason | Why it is built this way: fifteen decisions, each with its alternative and its reason | The architecture document records fifteen decisions, each with the alternative and the reason. Six of them shape what you have seen. The sweep runs as an Azure job rather than inside Snowflake, because a trial account has no network egress; on a paid account the first steps can move inside. Models go through Foundry rather than Snowflake Cortex: one platform for both providers, the agent, the guardrails and the evaluations. Calls are synchronous with bounded concurrency, because Claude on Foundry has no batch API and the comparison needs the same mode on both providers. Screening and extraction are single calls, not agents: one input, one structured output, nothing to decide. The agent's decision is the argument of a strict tool, because structured output through the framework's Anthropic client is not documented. And sessions are reused with a fresh thread per invocation, because hosted compute is billed per session, idle time included. | 52 |

| 30 | slide · agents and jobs: build-agent, deploy-agents with PLATFORM, kv-secrets, run-sweep-job | Build the image, deploy the hosted agents, put the keys in Key Vault, start a sweep | The hosted agents are one image built in the registry and deployed as versions, for either platform with a flag. The runner's keys go to Key Vault by file, never through a shell argument. A sweep starts as a job execution with its arguments, and refuses a full corpus unless you say so. Every step prints what it did, and teardown removes everything in Azure; the data platform keeps its own scripts. | 21 |
| 31 | slide · GitHub: CI on every push, manual deployment with OIDC, no stored cloud secrets; tests and lint | CI on every push; deployment is a manual workflow with OIDC and no stored secrets | The repository runs tests and lint on every push. Deployment is a manual GitHub workflow that logs into Azure with OpenID Connect, so no cloud secret is stored; the only secret is the parameters file. Sixty-nine unit tests cover the rules, the findings, the local schema and the Databricks store without touching the cloud. | 18 |
| 32 | slide · local development: DuckDB backend, make stage-data, synthetic data generator, snapshot, compare | Develop and evaluate on a laptop: the same runner over a local file | You do not need a cloud data platform to work on it. The runner has a DuckDB backend over the same Parquet files and synthetic records, so screening, extraction, rules, matching and findings run on a laptop; only the models are remote. A cloud run can be snapshotted into a local file, then evaluated and compared with another run with the same tools. | 20 |

## Part 4 · How it is measured (about 2 min 30)

| # | Screen | Caption | Narration | ~s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 33 | slide · synthetic data design: 13 scenario kinds, 5 channels, anchored on FAERS cases, two generators, a validator | 450 synthetic records with known answers, anchored on real cases, validated | There is no public dataset for this job, so the evaluation set was built scenario first: four hundred and fifty records across thirteen scenario kinds and five channels, each with its ground truth, ninety-three anchored on real FAERS cases so that lookalikes are real. Half the texts were written by GPT and half by Claude, so neither grades its own prose. The generator only writes the prose: the planted facts must appear verbatim, a wrong product or a contradicted causality rejects the text, and every record carries the hash of its scenario, which CI re-checks. Licences were checked twice, for the dataset and for the text underneath: two public forum corpora were rejected for lack of commercial rights, which is why the forum posts are synthetic. | 44 |
| 34 | shot · 08 Foundry evaluation report | Custom evaluators in Foundry grade routing, matching and causality | Three custom code evaluators are registered in the Foundry project and grade every run: routing, matching outcome and causality statement. The same functions run on the laptop, so a number in a document can always be reproduced. | 20 |
| 35 | slide · results table round 3, both providers, plus latency and cost | Measured, not assumed | On the same records: screening recall of ninety-seven percent or better for both providers, with at most one false positive in four hundred and fifty; extraction valid on every record with Claude and on all but two with GPT; matching eighty-four percent with GPT and ninety-five with Claude; and a full sweep costs about eleven dollars in model calls either way, measured for screening and extraction, estimated for the agent. | 28 |
| 36 | slide · same code, two hosting paths: Anthropic API vs Claude on Foundry; the recall loss is a format failure | Same code through Anthropic's API and through Foundry: within half a point | The same Claude code was run through Anthropic's own API and through Foundry, and the two are within half a point on every metric: matching ninety-five point six against ninety-five point four, recall ninety-six point eight against ninety-seven point six. The hosting path does not change quality; what Foundry adds is Entra ID, EU billing and the audit trail in the data platform. And Claude's recall loss is a format failure, not a judgement failure: fourteen records answered with invalid JSON twice and were logged but not routed. The fix is a retry on the large model, not a new prompt. | 36 |
| 37 | slide · what the numbers do not say: scale of proof, tier fairness, synthetic text | What the numbers do not say | And what they do not say: the texts are synthetic, GPT ran on a small model throughout while Claude matched with its large one, and the hosted path is proven on samples. The business case keeps those caveats next to the figures. | 20 |

## Part 5 · What it is worth, and the close (about 3 min)

| # | Screen | Caption | Narration | ~s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 38 | slide · the value model: manual vs sweep hours, known-record accuracy, cadence | The business case: 327 hours a month by hand, 24 to 39 with the sweep | The business case works a mid-size example: eight thousand records a month across the five channels, three percent of them with something for pharmacovigilance. Re-screening them by hand is three hundred and twenty-seven hours a month, about two people. With the sweep, the reviewer's time is thirty-nine hours with GPT or twenty-four with Claude, and the model cost is about fifteen dollars a month; people are ninety percent of the run-rate. The difference between providers is accuracy on records pharmacovigilance already holds: sixty percent against eighty-six, which is what decides how many findings a reviewer has to dismiss. And cadence matters: a weekly sweep finds an unforwarded serious case with at least eight of its fifteen days left; a monthly one finds most of them after the deadline, at the same model cost. | 50 |
| 39 | slide · data residency and the three provider options, the decision asked of the sponsor | Residency: three options, and the decision the sponsor is asked to make | Residency is settled per option, not assumed. Claude on Foundry is offered as Global Standard or in a US data zone only; its operational data is stored outside Azure, with Anthropic as an independent processor. So the business case puts three options in front of the sponsor. A: GPT throughout, in the EU data zone, the simplest residency and the most review hours. B: Claude throughout, the best measured matching, with a transfer assessment or pseudonymisation. C: GPT screens and extracts in the EU and Claude matches pseudonymised candidates, the highest recall with the best matching, at the price of two providers to validate. And it asks for three decisions: approve the pilot on a named channel, set the residency position, and name the QPPV delegate and the QA contact. | 48 |
| 40 | slide · path to production: connectors, network isolation, MedDRA, validation, pilot | From proof of concept to production | A production programme would add real connectors and a real safety database extract, network isolation, licensed MedDRA coding, computer system validation proportionate to risk, and a pilot with real reviewers to measure the business KPIs this proof of concept only simulates. | 22 |
